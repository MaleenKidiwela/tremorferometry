Part ALFE behaviour from the extended catalogues

1

Each ETS slips the fault in a different pattern; "set by how far the front gets" holds only for the half-island episodes

provisional

A family's share of activity changes from one ETS to the next more than counting noise allows. How much tremor was nearby does not explain it. The changes are shared by families within about 10–20 km and vanish beyond 20–40 km, in both regions.

58 / 89NW families vary beyond chance (SVI 27 / 75)
0.54 → 0.18SVI correlation, < 3 km to 10–20 km (counted once)
p 0.001distance effect, both regions, every cut-off

Why? Mostly how far the slip front gets. A patch inside the stretch the tremor front covered fires far more than usual. That alone explains about half the patch-to-patch differences within an episode (R² 0.53 Vancouver Island, 0.50 Washington; all front terms 0.62 / 0.61). On Vancouver Island, the shorter the front's path, the more uneven the episode, perfectly ranked over all 10 big ETS. Time since a patch last slipped does not predict a stronger patch (p 0.07 / 0.11), so there is no per-patch slip budget.

Redone on our own 2003–2026 record: patterns still differ between episodes far beyond counting noise (χ²/df 38 in 2003–13), and neighbouring families within ~10 km still move together in both decades. But "set by front reach" (R² 0.53 over 2003–26) rests on the five 2019–22 episodes that covered only half the island. All ten 2003–13 big ETS covered the whole area, and there front terms explain almost nothing (R² ≈ 0.06): why those patterns differ is unexplained. Turn-taking is absent in 2003–13.

Holds at P ≥ 0.5, 0.75 and 0.85, with bad episodes removed, and with duplicate events counted once. Washington's part is partly station-related. Vancouver Island 2015–26 also shows turn-taking (a patch strong in one big ETS tends to be weak in the next, p 0.001), but not in Washington or in Bostock's 2003–2013 catalogue, so it is likely how recent fronts happened to alternate.

2

Patches switch on for 2–3 days and stop, in a band of fixed width for 23 years

solid

A patch turns on when the tremor front reaches it, fires in hour-long bursts, and switches off within a day or two of its peak. There is no slow, aftershock-like fading. In Washington, patches stay on longer when the front moves slower: an active band about 15 km wide travelling with the front.

23 / 31SVI patch-episodes: plateau-then-stop beats decay (NW 17 / 28)
~70 %of LFEs inside hour-scale bursts (25 % when shuffled)

The active band behind the front has the same width in every big ETS from 2003 to 2026. Band width = front speed × how long patches stay on. It is about 15 km beneath Vancouver Island (Bostock 2003–2013: 14.7 km, varying only 8 % between episodes; ours 2014–2026: 15.6 km) and about 11–12 km in Washington (2007–2014: 10.6 km; 2015–2026: 11.8 km). Slower fronts keep patches on longer, which is what holds the width fixed (ρ −0.90 Washington, −0.83 Vancouver Island).

Deep patches switch off faster beneath Vancouver Island in Bostock's catalogue (2.3 vs 3.5 days, p 0.0004); same sign in ours but not significant. The Vancouver Island vs Washington width difference is not robust. Random thinning to our count needed a 15-event floor (post hoc).

3

Between ETS, only the deepest patches fire, in bursts, in both regions

solid

Outside every episode, with no tremor within 30 km, LFEs still occur. They come from families deeper than their ETS share predicts and cluster on a few days. Their timing gives no warning of the next ETS.

76 vs 3SVI between-episode LFEs vs scrambled control
220 vs 13Washington 2015–26 vs both controls
39.2 vs 35.4 kmSVI depth vs expected (WA 40.2 vs 32.3), p < 0.0001

Are these small slow slips? Partly. About half of the burst days fall in small tremor bursts within 30 km (Southern Vancouver Island 53 % vs 4 % on random days; Washington 33 % vs 4 %), which points to small slow slip. The tremor-free deep bursts on Vancouver Island have no tremor link at any distance. Bursts last hours, involve 2–3 families about 10 km apart, and recur at the same deep patches for over a decade, at random intervals. GPS cannot see slips this small (bound Mw ≤ 6). Borehole strainmeters can: stacked over 95 tremor-free deep bursts on 20 strainmeters they show no slip, bounding each burst at Mw ≤ 4.7 on average. The same stack sees big ETS (z 22), the 5–20-day minor episodes (z 14) and an injected Mw 5.2 every time. So tremor-free deep bursts are either not slow slips or slips smaller than Mw ~4.7, which rules out the central slow-slip scaling rate for bursts of this length.

Now back to 2003 (23-year record): the 2003–2013 backward catalogue shows the same thing: 109 tremor-free between-episode LFEs vs 9 in the scrambled control, deeper than ETS LFEs (p < 0.0001), bursty (p 0.001), from the same deep groups as 2014–26 (ρ 0.74, p 0.003). Several early-2006 bursts are deep bursts with local tremor.

No ramp-up before the next ETS in either region, now also tested over 2003–2026. The "quiet month after each ETS" is covered in finding 4. Between-episode rates before 2010 are compared only as presence and excess over the scrambled control, not as trends across network changes.

4

After a big ETS the deep zone goes quiet for 1–2 months; a year-long rebuild is not supported

provisional

In both regions, and in PNSN deep tremor, deep activity runs at about a tenth of its later rate for the first one to two months after a big ETS. The idea of a slow rebuild lasting a year or more did not survive Washington's 19-year record. Washington's deep activity peaks in a few huge bursts 5–8 months after a big ETS, then drops.

23-year record: in the deep family groups, 2003–2013 shows the same drop (1–13 % of the later level for ~60 days), but alone it misses the bar (p 0.059); 2003–2026 together passes (p 0.001). The other test (share of all tremor-free between-episode LFEs in the first 30 days) goes the wrong way in 2003–2013 (0.20 vs 0.13 shuffled), likely because shallow ETS-tail events after Bostock's window ends count as "between episodes"; the 2003–13 scrambled control also dips after ETS. So: stronger, still provisional.

Pre-registered verdict in Washington: not supported (p 0.09; four burst days hold 48 % of 2015–26 deep events). Counting days rather than events keeps the quiet month in Southern Vancouver Island (~53-day recovery) but removes it in Washington. A post-ETS quiet period looks real; its length and strength are not pinned down.

5

Vancouver Island and Washington ETS come in pairs, with no fixed leader

solid

Every big ETS is a front moving ~5 km per day along the coast. Fronts cross the border three ways: Washington to the Island, the Island to Washington, or starting near the Strait and splitting. Paired episodes occur far more often than chance, a few days apart, and the order does not repeat.

13 vs 4.7paired episodes vs chance (p 0.008)
8 of 13Washington first; median delay 4 days (1–14)
13.5 / 14.3 momedian ETS interval, NW / SVI; no drift 2010–2026

23-year record: 2003–2013 intervals average 14.1 months (CV 0.13), as regular as later. "Bigger episode, longer wait" holds with GNSS slip size (ρ 0.80) but not with LFE-based episode sizes.

Mostly confirms known behaviour (fronts, ~14-month recurrence). New is the family-level view over 2015–2026 and the lack of a consistent leader.

7

The tidal response builds up over 3–4 days at each patch, the same way for 23 years

solid

LFEs fire more when tidal shear pushes the fault toward thrust slip, reproducing Royer et al. (2015) on Bostock's catalogue with our tide model (ocean loading dominates here). At each patch the response starts near zero when activity begins, peaks after 3–4 days, then fades. Its timing shifts from the fastest-rising stress to the stress peak. Bostock's 2003–2013 catalogue and our 2014–2026 extension show the same build-up, and the overall response did not change over 23 years.

8

Slip fronts stop where the fault slipped recently

provisional

Across the whole margin (PNSN tremor 2010–2026, 475 episodes), a front is more likely to stop at a stretch that had tremor within the last month. Stretches quiet for more than three months let fronts through. Recently slipped ground acts like a barrier. The opposite idea, that fronts stop where the fault has been quiet longest, is rejected.

Pre-registered verdict PARTIAL: the Vancouver Island LFE check has only 6 front stops (wrong raw sign); adding 2003–2013 brings only 1 more stop (9 in all, below the 20 needed), so it stays undecided, and Bostock's 10 ETS cover every bin so they cannot test arrest. The Strait of Juan de Fuca is not a boundary: starts and stops there match chance.

9

Fast tremor bursts, reversals included, fire when tides push the fault up-dip, in both decades; the "big LFEs" in them belong to busy hours

provisional

Rapid tremor reversals are short bursts that run back through the already-slipped zone at a few km per hour. An automatic, control-tested finder recovers them in Bostock's 2003–2013 catalogue: 88 reversals, 12 days into an episode on average, 3–4 km/h over ~15 km, like Royer et al. 2015. Shuffling event times removes them. In both decades they happen while tides push the fault up-dip, and they are strongest on the shallow side. Bostock 2015 said reversals hold the biggest LFEs. Here, every LFE firing in those same hours is just as big, so size goes with busy hours, not with reversals.

7 of 8 vs 40 %2014–26 reversals wholly in positive up-dip tidal shear vs expected (p 0.009); 2003–13: 76 % vs 31 %
88 vs 1.6reversals in Bostock 2003–13 vs time-shuffled control; 88 run backward vs 31 forward
+0.09 vs +0.08magnitude above family median: reversal LFEs vs other LFEs in the same hours (difference p 0.22)

PNSN tremor 2010–2026 (Q8b) gives 1–2 reversals per big ETS in every region (Vancouver Island 42, Washington-NW 61, Washington-SW 36; 4–5 km/h, ~21 km, ~1.5 h), almost none after shuffling times. The tidal preference holds there too, but forward-moving and non-migrating tremor bursts are just as tidal: the tide sets when fast bursts happen, not specifically their direction.

Our own Vancouver Island candidates, kept with probability equal to their confidence P (Q8c), give 2.5 reversals per big ETS in 2014–2026: as many as Bostock's 2003–2013 catalogue gives at the same density (2.3). Reversals are as common as before. Real ones fall in up-dip tidal shear 78 % of the time vs 36 % expected; reversals found in the scrambled control do not (35 % vs 42 %).

The finder was tuned after seeing Royer's list: 58 % one-to-one match vs 34 % by chance (pre-registered bar 70 % not met). Our 2014–26 catalogues have ~30× fewer events per episode than Bostock's (median 640 vs 19,700), so they catch only ~4 % of reversals: 8 in SVI, 6 in Washington-NW (the Washington ones are not separable from their control). Busy-hour sizes could partly be overlapping LFEs. Tremor and LFE reversals agree only weakly (27 % matched vs 14 % chance, 2010–13); tremor 2014–26 tidal share 56 % vs 39 % (p 0.055); tremor locations are too rough to test up-dip or repeat sites.
